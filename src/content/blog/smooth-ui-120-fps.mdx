Scrolling and animation feel different at 30 and 120 frames per second. Files 0.1.33 lets you choose the frame-rate limit and see what the app actually draws, instead of guessing from how the interface feels.

## Choose your frame rate

Open **Settings → General → Frame rate** and pick **24, 30, 45, 60, 90 or 120 FPS**. The default is 30 FPS. Higher values allow smoother scrolling and animations; lower ones can reduce the work Files does.

The display sets the upper bound. On a 60 Hz monitor, choosing 90 or 120 FPS will not produce more than 60 presented frames per second. To see 120 FPS, you need a display currently running at 120 Hz or more.

## Why 90 FPS could look like 30

A timer that wakes up every 11 ms does not line up with a 60 Hz display's frames, which arrive every 16.67 ms. It can miss the next presentation opportunity and wait for the following one. The result is only about 30 FPS, despite the higher setting.

For 60, 90 and 120 FPS, Files now asks the window system for the next animation frame. Drawing follows the display instead of fighting it with a separate timer. The lower 24, 30 and 45 FPS limits still use timed updates.

Windows no longer drop to 30 FPS merely because they are inactive. This matters when DevTools or Music is open beside the main Files window.

## See the real numbers

Turn on **Settings → Developer → Show FPS counter**. DevTools then reports the frames actually presented, including frame-time statistics — not the value selected in Settings.

You can **detach DevTools into its own window** and keep it beside Files while scrolling, opening folders or playing animations. That makes it easier to distinguish a frame-rate limit from work that makes individual frames expensive.

<Callout>
The FPS counter is a diagnostic tool. Turn off **Show FPS counter** when you no longer need it: continuous frame measurement also keeps continuous redraws active.
</Callout>

The frame-rate control, FPS counter and detachable DevTools are available in [Files 0.1.33](/blog/files-0-1-33).
